Netflix’s reboot of the Queer Eye franchise has been met with widespread critical acclaim, but it’s also generated some controversy. Some have accused the show of being dated and tone-deaf, while others have expressed skepticism about some of the experts’ credentials (yes, Antoni — we’re looking at you). But there’s one thing most Queer Eye viewers can agree on: the show has drastically changed the lives of the eight men who were made over.

Throughout the series, the Fab Five encourage their predominantly straight male subjects to embrace their emotions, reconnect with their partners, and maybe even try a face mask or two. On the show, these men, whom the cast affectionately refers to as "heroes," opened up about their personal struggles with male identity with refreshing candor, prompting reviewers to applaud the show for being an “antidote” to toxic masculinity.

We caught up with two of these heroes: Neal Reddy (episode two) and Anthony "AJ" Brown (episode four), to discuss the lasting impact of their Queer Eye experiences, and how the show helped shape their views of being a man in today's world.

Neal Reddy

Before Reddy appeared on Queer Eye, he was an introvert who used jokes to deflect from serious conversations. His living situation didn’t help matters: his apartment barely had any furniture and it was in total chaos, which meant he rarely had visitors (and let’s not even mention the Grizzly Adams beard).

"Why does taking care of yourself have to be this thing you're made fun of for?"

The Fab Five made over his appearance, cutting his hair and trimming his beard to a manageable length. More importantly, however, they taught him to be more in touch with his emotions and less cut off from the outside world.

“I feel fully confident in business meetings. I'm way more engaged. I just feel like I'm a part of the team and people respect what I have to say more because I feel better about what I'm saying because of how I'm presenting myself," says Reddy, an Atlanta-based developer. "And it's not just new clothes. It's my attitude, too. And that’s kind of what they helped me with."

Appearing on the show also helped Reddy realize the value of self-care, which he’d previously dismissed as a non-masculine pursuit.

"There was a time a few years ago where if you took care of yourself, people called you a metrosexual. It was a derogatory thing," he reflects. "It's different now. Maybe you're not being metrosexual; you're just taking care of yourself. Why does it have to be this thing you get made fun of for?"

Since the show, Reddy has started working out regularly. He also started dating a woman, and while it ultimately didn't work out, he credits the Fab Five with teaching him to be more open and vulnerable in that relationship than in any of his previous ones.

"I just started it out in a different, healthy way. I was communicating, and I was doing the right things," Reddy says. "We ended things, but I felt good about the relationship, and that wouldn't have happened without the show."

Ultimately, Reddy says being on Queer Eye taught him to come to terms with outdated versions of masculinity.

"I think being a man just means being open to let go of some of these things that we as men have held onto for so long,” he says. “Part of what I held on to for so long was the idea that being a man means you gotta be on your own, and if something happens you don't cry, and you gotta be tough. And I held on to that stereotype... now, I think being a man now is not that at all. It's ever-changing, ever-evolving. It's not just one thing."

Anthony "A.J." Brown

Brown's journey on the show was the most emotional of the season. Prior to the show, Brown was terrified of coming out to many of the people in his life, including his stepmother. He often dressed in baggy, unflattering clothes because he was worried about appearing too "feminine," and although he was in a committed relationship with his boyfriend Andre, few people knew about it, as Brown refused to use words like "he" and "him" when referring to his significant other.

"I had been living like that for a while so I got used to the pronoun game, using 'they' and 'them.' It had become so natural, [but] it was exhausting," Brown told MensHealth.com. "Now I'm breaking out of that."

"Every time I watch the episode, it helps me connect [with my father] again."

At the end of the episode, Brown decided to finally come out to his stepmother by reading her a heartfelt letter he'd addressed to his late father, leaving everyone (including viewers) in tears. "I watched the episode and I cried again," Brown said. "Every time I watch it, it helps me connect [with my father] again and it feels like a big relief."

Since the show debuted, Brown has gotten engaged to his boyfriend Andre, prompting the Fab Five to congratulate him on Twitter. He also continues to wear stylish clothes that flatter his (surprisingly jacked, as the Fab Five pointed out) body. When coworkers first saw his new wardrobe, "they were like, 'What?! Have you been working out?'" he recalls. "I'm like, 'No, my clothes are just fitting.'" (As you can see below, he's probably being modest.)

As he looks to his future with his now-fiancé, Brown looks back on his experience with Queer Eye as forcing him to redefine what it means to be a man. For him, it's about living up to the man his late father raised him to be.

"It means taking care of your responsibilities, even when it's hard to do. It means being true to yourself, and not lying to people," he says.
