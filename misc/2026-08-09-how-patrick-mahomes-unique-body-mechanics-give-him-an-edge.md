# How Patrick Mahomes' Unique Body Mechanics Give Him an Edge

In just three full seasons as a starting quarterback, Kansas City Chiefs superstar Patrick Mahomes has thrown 114 touchdown passes and racked up 14,152 passing yards, establishing himself as one of the NFL’s most dominant signal-callers. He’s just the third quarterback in history (after Tom Brady and Peyton Manning) to throw for 50 TDs and win MVP in the same season, and no quarterback has reached 100 career TD passes more quickly.

[Mahomes' Unique](https://skin.99ht99.com/blog/8006080081.html)

But none of this means Mahomes has a prototypical quarterback body. Truth be told, Mahomes is far from the cyborg QB that you’d build in a lab, says his trainer, Bobby Stroupe. “Let’s just be honest: He’s not a Greek god,” says Stroupe. “He’s not an aesthetic, dominating figure. If you look at his anatomical setup . . . his body is built like a reverse centaur. He has a really big upper body, and a triangular, smaller lower body.”

Translation: Mahomes is built differently than your signal-calling norm. The standard NFL quarterbacking prototype is built the other way around. From Cam Newton to Brady to Lamar Jackson, most quarterbacks feature strong, powerful lower bodies. This lets them set their feet in the ground, step forward, capturing glute and hamstring power, and then rotate through their hips, adding a bit of rotational oomph to every pass. That blend of rotational power through the torso plus lower-body power helps fuel deep balls and bullet passes alike.

At 6-3, 230 pounds, Mahomes lacks that gift, even if he does consistently work to build leg strength. And that means he can’t rely on leg drive to push the ball downfield. So how does he fling all those picture-perfect bombs to Tyreek Hill and Travis Kelce?

The answer: A uniquely ultra-bendy spine that, if scouts start to pay closer attention, might help identify other unique quarterbacking talents. Mahomes has perhaps the most unique gift a QB could have, what Stroupe calls “the most athletic spine I’ve ever seen.” Turns out, Mahomes can explosively twist and turn his upper body any which way.

Stroupe noticed this early on when training Mahomes. A young Mahomes didn’t necessarily dominate classic football drills, didn’t bench the biggest weights or run the fastest. But if you put a medball in his hands and asked him to fling it, whether rotationally or overhead, he posted explosive stats. Mahomes rotates his shoulders as quickly and efficiently as an athlete can, and he can rotate his hips one way and his shoulders another.

Mahomes can rely on this upper body movement to make just about any throw, and it gives him a freedom to move and pass that few QBs possess. All those quarterbacks who rely on their lower bodies to throw must set their feet, if only for a moment, to capitalize on that lower-body power. To some extent, Mahomes doesn’t need to focus on lower body positioning, since he’ll create enough explosion through his spine to slingshot passes to their targets.

He presents a new and unique template for a quarterback, capable of classic passes and dangerous cross-body throws, all delivered with a wrist-flick and some spinal power. Mahomes can freewheel like few others, and there’s never been a need to adjust his throwing motion, says Stroupe.

And why mess with what works? Mahomes’ job isn’t to look prototypical. It’s to find ways to get his passes to his targets, whether he’s using an overhand throwing motion or a sidearm motion, or throwing against his body. He needs to be able to adjust to any situation, and he can do that.

“His job is to see someone he’s throwing to,” says Stroupe. “Solve a problem.”
